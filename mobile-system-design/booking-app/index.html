Design a hotel or ride booking app (Android and iOS): holds, payments and recovery

How to read this. This page is the 45 minutes, in the order I would spend them. The schema, REST calls, code, search, deep links and platform tables are in the build guide. The follow-up questions below are my reconstruction of the challenges that public prep material reports: retries, offline use, lost responses and rollout. They are not transcripts of real rounds.

TL;DR. My answer: the server owns the money, the rooms, the time and the booking state. The phone keeps one saved attempt and a key (a unique ID that lets the server spot a repeat request). The phone makes the key before the first send. After an interruption (a timeout, a bank page, or the system stopping the app), the phone asks the server what happened. It does not guess.

1. Scope and constraints (minutes 0 to 4)

In short: Hotels only. Booking works online only. Rides wait until the interviewer asks.

A prompt I would expect: "Design a hotel booking app." I ask five questions and give my default for each:

  • Hotels only, or rides too? Hotels. I add rides later if you ask.
  • Hold a room during checkout? Yes, for a short time. The server keeps the room for the user. The length of the hold is a product choice.
  • Which payments? Cards and wallets. The app sends a token (a code that stands for the card). The bank may also ask for an extra check (3-D Secure).
  • Book offline? No. Saved bookings can be read offline.
  • Search? Search exists and returns quotes (prices with an ID and a time).

Your decision. The scope question I expect: "Add ride requests too." Do you include them, or leave them for later?

First idea, and why it fails. Say yes and share the checkout. But a ride adds driver matching and a live driver stream. Time runs out before I draw the recovery path, and that path touches money.

What I would say.

Say this. "Hotels are the whole design. A ride adds matching and a live marker, so I will leave it for later. The key and the lookup carry over. Tell me if you want rides earlier."

The follow-up question I expect: "It is the same checkout with a map."

Mostly, yes. The key and the lookup carry over. The live stream is new, and it returns as follow-up 2.

2. Data and contracts (minutes 4 to 14)

In short: The server owns rooms, price, time and state. The phone keeps one attempt record.

Your decision. The user taps Pay with no signal. Do you queue the booking like a chat message, or refuse it?

First idea, and why it fails. Queue it as pending, send it later, and show a confirmation. This fails when the room sells while the phone is offline. The app then confirmed something that cannot happen.

What I would say. Booking works online only, and I reject the queue. Android's offline-first guide says this suits writes that need near real time, such as a bank transfer (guide). I turn off the button and show the reason.

The follow-up question I expect: "The user taps Pay in a tunnel, and the signal drops after the tap."

The attempt record is already on disk. When the signal returns, I look up the booking by its key. If the booking exists, I use it. If not, I send the request again with the same key.

A price is a quote with an ID and a time. The hold request carries the quote ID. If the price changed, the server answers with a state, and the user must accept the new price. Dates are calendar dates in the hotel's time zone. Money is an amount in minor units (such as cents) plus a currency. The client never adds money up.

The contract has three calls:

  • Hold: quoteId in. holdId and a duration out.
  • Pay: holdId, a wallet or card token and an Idempotency-Key header in. A booking with a version out.
  • Lookup: find a booking by the key that created it.

The states run: HELD, PAYMENT_REQUIRED, ACTION_REQUIRED, PROCESSING, CONFIRMED. Before that, a booking can end as EXPIRED or FAILED. After it, a booking can become CANCELLED. The build guide has the schema and the REST sketch.

3. Client structure (minutes 14 to 22)

In short: The screen only watches. The pay call and the attempt record live below the screen.

Your decision. Who owns the pay call? Choose from three owners:

  • The ViewModel of the screen (the class that holds the screen state).
  • A background job.
  • A scope that the repository owns (the repository is the class that reads and writes data).

First idea, and why it fails. The ViewModel starts the call in viewModelScope. But Android clears a ViewModel when its owner goes away, for example when its screen leaves the back stack, the list of opened screens (ViewModel). Then the call is cancelled, or its result is lost after the card was charged.

SavedStateHandle (a small saved store for screen state) survives when the system stops the app. It does not survive a force stop by the user. It holds only small values (saved state).

What I would say. Ownership goes below the screen.

  • A scope that the repository owns runs the call, so leaving the screen cancels nothing.
  • The attempt row holds the key and the status. I write it to Room (Android's on-device database) before the first send.
  • The screen watches the row and never changes the state itself.

I reject a persistent background job for the charge itself. The user is waiting, and WorkManager (Android's scheduler for work that survives restarts) is for work that must finish after the user leaves (persistent work). That is my reading. The repository scope still ends when the app ends, which is why the row exists.

The follow-up question I expect: "Where does the recovery lookup run?"

A plain call at startup is enough. It finds the open attempts and asks the server about each one.

  • A persistent job is the upgrade if recovery must run without the app opening.

In Android's terms, the screen and the ViewModel are the UI layer, and the repositories are the data layer. I add a domain layer only when several ViewModels share logic or one ViewModel grows too complex (recommendations). Three rules:

  • The ViewModel never calls the API. It asks the repository.
  • The attempt row is written before the first send.
  • The client never changes the booking state itself.

4. Make the hard part work (minutes 22 to 40)

In short: One key per attempt, saved first. The bank page is only a hint. The server owns the clock.

4.1 The charge that may or may not have happened (minutes 22 to 32)

Your decision. The pay call times out. You do not know whether the card was charged. What do you send next?

First idea, and why it fails. Send the pay call again, and guard it with a disabled button. This has two problems:

  • The server charged the card and the response was lost. A retry with a new key is a second charge.
  • The disabled button lives in memory, and a stopped app forgets it.

What I would say.

Say this. "I make one key per attempt. I write it to Room before the first send. After a timeout, I look it up by that key. If the booking exists, I use it. If not, I send again with the same key, so the server can charge only once. A return from a bank page or wallet is only a hint. I move to processing and ask the server."

I reject a new key for each tap. Stripe's page says that in API v1 the server saves the first result for a key and returns it on retries (idempotent requests). A request is idempotent when doing it twice has the same result as doing it once. That only works when the key is the same.

The follow-up question I expect: "The user closes the bank page and taps Pay again. Same key?"

That is a new attempt, so I check the old one first.

  • Look up the old key. If a booking exists, use it.
  • Providers differ on key rules, so I follow the rules of mine.
  • Use a new key only after I know the old attempt is dead.

The follow-up question I expect: "The bank page says done, then the app restarts. What do you show?"

I show processing, then the answer from the server. Stripe's page says to get the updated PaymentIntent (Stripe's record of one payment) after the challenge (3-D Secure). The result of the redirect is only a hint.

4.2 The hold and whose clock (minutes 32 to 40)

Your decision. How does the screen know when the hold ends? Do you compare a stored end time with the clock of the device, or count down from a duration?

First idea, and why it fails. Compare a stored end time with the clock of the device. A phone with a clock 20 minutes fast shows a hold that has already ended. If the user changes the date, the countdown jumps.

What I would say.

Say this. "The server sends a duration. Before the request leaves, I note the time on a monotonic clock, a clock that ignores date changes. I count down from that time. Delay on the network makes the screen show a little less time than the server grants, never more. At zero, I lock Pay and read the hold again, because the server may have extended it."

I reject the device clock for this. The server checks the hold again when the user pays.

The follow-up question I expect: "The hold ends while the bank page is open."

The server decides.

  • It returns EXPIRED, or it cancels the payment by its own rules.
  • The client shows that state, with an action to check again.

4.3 Where iOS changes the design

The architecture is the same, but the guarantees are different. The Swift delegate (the object that handles the sheet events) is in the build guide.

  • The Apple Pay sheet waits for my handler, so the server call stays short. A timeout means the result is unknown. I fail the sheet and look the booking up by key.
  • The system may end the app while the user is in the bank page. I restore the attempt record at launch. When the app becomes active again, it starts the lookup.
  • iOS has no equivalent of SavedStateHandle, so the attempt record goes to storage from the first send.

5. Break it, ship it, roll it back (minutes 40 to 45)

In short: Every failure ends in a booking, a clear error, or a lookup. It never ends in a guess.

Release reality:

  • Store rollouts can pause, but they do not recall. A halted Play rollout reaches no new users, and users who have the build keep it (Play Console). Apple's phased release runs seven days and can pause for 30 days in total (Apple). It does not mention reverting installed copies.
  • Old versions stay installed, so the server keeps accepting the old request and the key header.
  • The new payment path ships behind a remote flag (a setting the server can change). This is my kill switch, and it is the only rollback that reaches installed apps. The rollback path must still check the key before it charges.
  • A change to the attempt table, or a new state that old clients do not know, cannot be flagged. It ships one release earlier.

Say this. "I cannot recall a bad build, so the rollback is the flag plus a fixed build. I would watch the rate of unknown outcomes and the reports of duplicate charges, not conversion. I left out rides, modify and cancel, and two devices."

6. Follow-ups that change a constraint

In short: Each follow-up changes one constraint. A strong answer says what breaks.

These are practice prompts, not claims about real rounds.

  1. The user taps Pay in a tunnel and stays offline for 30 minutes. Strong: the attempt row is on disk, and the screen says why Pay is off. When the signal returns, look up by key before any new send, and expect the hold to have ended. Weak: "Queue the booking and sync later."
  2. Add ride requests with a live driver marker. Strong: the same key and lookup, and a socket (an open two-way connection) only while the trip screen is visible. On reconnect, fetch the latest trip state by ID instead of replaying the stream. Weak: reuse the hotel screen with a map.
  3. Hold on the phone, pay on a laptop; also modify and cancel. Strong: server state is the truth, trips sync by version, and a hold belongs to the user. Modify and cancel reuse the key pattern with a version check. Weak: syncing local rows between devices.
  4. A security review of card data and deep links (links that open a screen in the app). Strong: the app handles only a token, which I believe keeps raw card numbers out of my systems. PCI scope (the card industry's security rules) is my understanding, so confirm it with the provider. Check every link parameter. Weak: "We store the card encrypted."
  5. After a release, "charged but no booking" reports double. Strong: check the rate of unknown outcomes, switch the flag to the old path, and confirm that this path checks the key. Then ship a fix. Weak: "Roll back the release."
  6. The server adds a booking state, and three older app versions are live. Strong: for an unknown state, the app asks the server, shows the last known state and asks the user to update. Weak: assuming everyone updates.

7. What changes by level

In short: Levels differ in who raises the problem, and whether a rollout plan comes with it.

These levels are my own reading of the bar, drawn from vendor and prep material. They are not the rubric of any company.

  • Mid-level: a working flow, error states, cached trips, and Pay disabled while the request is sending. I want to hear: "I disable Pay while the request is in flight and show the error from the server."
  • Senior: the saved key with a lookup before any new send, a server duration on a monotonic clock, and states driven by the server. It also has reconciliation after the app stops (comparing the phone with the server and fixing differences). I want to hear: "I write the key to Room before the first send, so after a crash I look the booking up instead of paying twice."
  • Staff: reconciliation across the hold, payment and booking services, flags, and metrics (staff guide). I want to hear: "I would ship the new payment path to 1 percent of users behind a flag. I would watch reports of duplicate charges, not conversion."

As I read it, at mid-level the interviewer raises the problem. At senior level the candidate does. At staff level the candidate arrives with a rollout plan.

Common weak answers. Twenty minutes on backend locks. A happy path with no failure behavior. The UI calling the network. No key before the first send. Trusting the bank redirect. No rollout plan. Designing search instead of checkout. I took them from public prep material. They are not a dataset.

Staff answer: the decision and the rollout

  • Decision: a booking state machine owned by the server, with the client as a view of it. The cost is more server work and a poll or push path.
  • Alternative: the client guesses the outcome from redirects and timers. It ships faster, and it charges twice on the first lost response.
  • Revisit trigger: if the rate of unknown outcomes stays high after lookup-before-send ships, look at provider events and server transitions first.
  • Rollout: the new path goes behind the section 5 flag, then a staged release.
  • Guardrail: reports of duplicate charges and "charged but no booking" contacts must not rise. A conversion win that moves them does not count.

Opinion vs verified: I read the Stripe, Android and store-rollout facts on official pages. The metrics, the rollout plan, the numbers and the level text are my opinion.
