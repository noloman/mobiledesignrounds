Design a stock ticker app (Android and iOS): streaming, conflation and resync

How to read this. This page is the 45 minutes, in the order I would spend them. Wire formats, code and platform tables are in the build guide. The pushes below are my reconstruction of the kinds of challenges public prep material reports (offline, retries, stale data, battery, rollout), not transcripts of real rounds.

TL;DR. My answer: the socket (a two-way connection) is the easy part. The hard part is deciding what the user may believe about a number. I stream only what is on screen, keep ticks in memory, render the latest value, and show how old every price is. Sequence numbers (a counter per symbol) repair gaps, and a heartbeat (a regular ping), not silence, decides when everything is stale.

1. Scope and constraints (minutes 0 to 4)

In short: Live means the rows on screen. Everything else gets a snapshot.

A prompt I would expect: "Design the screen in a trading app that shows live prices." I ask five things, with my default:

  • How many symbols? Twelve on screen, fifty watched, thousands searchable.
  • How live? Real time on screen, a snapshot elsewhere.
  • Which screens? A watchlist and a detail with a chart.
  • Client only? The client and the gateway contract, not the exchange side.
  • Offline? Always online: cached prices, marked stale.

Your call. Do you promise live prices for the whole symbol universe, or only for what the screen shows?

First instinct, and why it breaks. Subscribe to everything and let the UI pick the rows. With thousands of symbols and twelve visible, the radio and the data plan carry ticks nobody sees.

What I'd say.

Say it. "Live means the rows the user can see. The watchlist is capped at fifty and is the subscription set while on screen. Anything off screen gets one snapshot. Widgets, alerts and delayed licences are parked; tell me if you want one earlier."

The push I would expect:

"Traders want every watched symbol live, even off screen."

The cap is fifty. I still release a symbol when its screen goes away.

  • A hidden screen has no reader.

2. Data and contracts (minutes 4 to 14)

In short: Ticks stay in memory. Only the watchlist and last snapshot go to disk.

Your call. Do live ticks go through the database like the rest of the app, or stay in memory?

First instinct, and why it breaks. Write every tick to Room (Android's database library) and let the screen observe it, because the database is the source of truth. On a busy open that is hundreds of writes a second for values obsolete within a second, and the battery pays.

What I'd say. Ticks live in a conflated in-memory map (it keeps only the newest value per symbol). I persist only the watchlist and the last snapshot per symbol, a conscious exception to single source of truth that fits ephemeral data only.

The push I would expect:

"Why a WebSocket and not server-sent events?"

It depends on whether the symbol set changes while the screen is open. It does, as rows scroll, so subscribe and unsubscribe travel on the same pipe.

  • For a fixed set, server-sent events (a one-way stream over HTTP) would be simpler.
  • Polling is out: every answer is already old.
  • Polling also asks even when nothing changed.

The push I would expect:

"The process is killed. What is on screen at relaunch?"

The cached snapshot. Every row is marked stale with its snapshot time, until a fresh snapshot lands.

The contract in four lines:

  • Snapshot: latest quotes for a list of symbols, with sequence number and market state.
  • History: a range and a point count; the client appends live ticks to the last candle.
  • Socket: subscribe and unsubscribe up; a snapshot, then ticks with a per-symbol seq and server time, plus market-state messages, down.
  • Watchlist: an idempotent PUT per symbol.

The seq is a promise my gateway makes to each client, contiguous by design; an exchange feed does not. Chat's tolerates gaps (chat case).

3. Client structure (minutes 14 to 22)

In short: One stream owner in the data layer, not the ViewModel.

Your call. Who owns the socket and the subscriptions: the screen's ViewModel, an object in the data layer, a WorkManager job, or a foreground service?

First instinct, and why it breaks. The ViewModel. It holds the screen state, so it opens the socket in viewModelScope. But a ViewModel is cleared when its screen leaves the back stack (ViewModel). Two screens showing one symbol open two sockets with separate gap bugs.

What I'd say. One stream owner in the data layer, in a scope it controls, started on the first subscriber and stopped when the app leaves the foreground. Subscriptions are ref-counted and tied to visibility. I reject WorkManager. Android's background-work page points to it for tasks that must finish, and says coroutine work stops when the app does (background tasks). A screen of prices is the second kind.

The push I would expect:

"Keep it open in the background with a foreground service."

I would not. Android bars most background starts (restrictions).

  • It describes the type as immediate work the user can see.
  • A price screen nobody is looking at is neither.
  • Alerts go by push.

Screen and ViewModel are Android's UI layer; QuoteRepository is the data layer's entry point, with the REST client, stream owner and database behind it. I skip a domain layer until logic is shared across ViewModels (recommendations). Three rules:

  • The UI never touches the socket. It collects state.
  • The stream owner is the only writer of the in-memory book.
  • The ViewModel exposes one immutable uiState.

4. Make the hard part work (minutes 22 to 40)

In short: Render the latest value, never every tick. A heartbeat decides when prices are stale.

4.1 Render the latest, not every tick (minutes 22 to 31)

Your call. A busy symbol ticks many times a frame. Do you render every tick, sample on a timer, or apply every tick and render the latest?

First instinct, and why it breaks. A fixed sample(100) looks smooth. But its docs note the latest element is not emitted if it does not fit the sampling window (sample). The last tick of a burst can vanish, and the row sits on an old price until the next tick.

What I'd say.

Say it. "I apply every tick to an in-memory map and the screen reads the latest. A StateFlow conflates, so a slow collector gets the newest value. I don't sample prices, because sampling can drop the last value of a burst. Every row shows its age, so a number that stopped moving looks old."

The push I would expect:

"Why not have the server send fewer ticks?"

I ask for both.

  • A server cap saves the bytes.
  • Conflation protects the frame.

The push I would expect:

"And a screen reader user?"

I do not announce ticks. I announce only rare changes, such as market closed.

  • Android's guidance says not to use live regions (areas the screen reader speaks aloud) on frequently updating content (semantics).

4.2 When the link lies (minutes 31 to 40)

Your call. The connection drops, or stays open and delivers nothing. After it recovers, do you replay what you missed, take a snapshot, or carry on?

First instinct, and why it breaks. Trust the open socket and replay missed ticks after a reconnect. On a half-open connection (open but silent) no close event arrives and frames stop. Android's network-state page says a network behind a captive portal (a login page before internet access) has the internet capability but is not validated (network state). Nothing triggers replay, and it is wasted when only the latest price matters.

What I'd say.

Say it. "I ping every ten seconds. Two missed pongs (replies) mean the link is dead. I rebuild it, resubscribe the active symbols and take one snapshot each. A sequence gap on a symbol means one snapshot request, and I ignore its ticks until then. On a last price a missed tick heals itself. A gap matters for the live candle, whose high, low and volume are built from ticks."

I reject replay, and polling as a second code path for detecting a dead link.

The push I would expect:

"Chat replays after a drop. Why not here?"

A chat message is a separate fact, so losing one loses information. A price replaces the one before it, so the snapshot is my replay.

The push I would expect:

"An illiquid symbol has not ticked for ten minutes. Is it stale?"

No. Stale is a fact about the connection, so it keys on the heartbeat. The row shows its own age, and an old price on a healthy link is just old.

4.3 Where iOS changes the design

Same architecture, different primitives; the Swift is in the build guide.

  • Conflation is an explicit choice. AsyncStream buffers without limit unless told otherwise, so I pick newest-only.
  • Apple documents no reconnect for URLSessionWebSocketTask (Apple), so I rebuild and resubscribe myself. It does offer a ping.
  • I close the socket when the scene leaves .active. I have not confirmed what a WebSocket task may do in the background, so I do not rely on it.

5. Break it, ship it, roll it back (minutes 40 to 45)

In short: Every failure ends in an honest label and a fresh snapshot. The kill switch is a server flag.

Release reality:

  • A Play staged rollout can be halted, and users who already have the build keep it (Play Console). An App Store phased release can be paused for up to 30 days (Apple); the page does not mention reverting installed copies.
  • Old versions stay in the field, so the gateway keeps serving the old tick format.
  • The stream ships behind a server flag telling clients to stop streaming and refresh snapshots slowly: my kill switch, and the only rollback that reaches installed apps.
  • A watchlist schema change cannot be flagged off, so it ships one release ahead of the code that needs it.

Say it. "I can't recall a bad build, so the rollback is the flag plus a fixed build. I'd watch time to first price, resync rate, reconnects per session and battery per session. Left out: orders, news."

6. Follow-ups that change a constraint

In short: Each follow-up changes one constraint. A strong answer says what breaks first.

Practice prompts, not a claim about what any interviewer asks. Each changes a constraint.

  1. The market opens and 2,000 symbols move at once. Strong: name where it gives first (parsing, the map, the main thread, the server's send buffer), then pull conflation, per-row state and a server rate cap. Weak: "scale the server."
  2. A home screen widget and a watch face. Strong: neither holds a socket; they show snapshots on a timeline, and I read the platform refresh limits before promising a frequency. Weak: open a socket in the widget.
  3. The watchlist and alerts are edited on a phone and a tablet. Strong: the server wins, the list PUT is idempotent, an alert POST carries a clientId, and a push is a hint that loads a fresh snapshot. Weak: merge locally forever.
  4. Some users are licensed for delayed prices. Strong: the server tags every quote and the client labels it; a token refresh on the live socket keeps the subscriptions. Weak: the client decides.
  5. After a release the resync rate doubles and battery complaints rise. Strong: flip the flag to snapshot mode, halt the rollout, check the heartbeat interval and resubscribe storms. Weak: "roll back the release."
  6. The gateway changes the tick message and some users are on old builds. Strong: a version sent on subscribe, both shapes served, retirement through a minimum-version rule. Weak: force an update.

7. What changes by level

In short: Levels differ in who raises the problem: the interviewer, the candidate, or the candidate with a rollout plan.

These levels are my own reading of the bar, drawn from vendor and prep material (courses and books), not any company's rubric. The wording below is mine.

  • Mid-level: a WebSocket for prices, REST for history, decimal prices, throttled UI updates. I want to hear: "Every price shows how old it is."
  • Senior: visible-only subscriptions, conflation with a reason, seq gaps repaired by snapshot, a heartbeat for the half-open link, the socket closed in the background. Raised unasked. I want to hear: "Here is what the user may believe when the link lies."
  • Staff: which surfaces may hold a socket at all, one shared stream owner, what the kill switch turns off, the feed's cost, and metrics (staff guide). I want to hear: "Here is the guardrail that halts the release."

Common weak answers. Rendering every tick. Polling for prices. A Double for money. No stale or closed state. An hour on server fan-out. Naming a WebSocket library, not what happens when it drops. No rollout plan. Treating a quiet symbol as a dead connection. These repeat across public prep material; they are not a dataset.

Staff answer: the decision and the rollout

Decision: one shared stream module owns the socket, the subscription counts and the seq checks. Each surface asks it for symbols instead of opening its own connection. Alternative: a socket per surface, simpler to start, but it multiplies connections and gap bugs. Revisit trigger: a surface with a different freshness need, such as a widget or watch, where snapshots are enough and the module is dead weight.

Rollout: the new stream goes behind the section 5 flag, then a staged release. Guardrail: the resync rate must not rise, and dropped frames on the watchlist must not worsen.

Opinion vs verified: the platform and store facts were read from official pages. The module split, metrics, rollback plan, numbers and level text are my opinion.
