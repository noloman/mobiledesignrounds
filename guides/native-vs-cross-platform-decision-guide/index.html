Decision (worked example, not your company). A company has two mature native apps, about forty mobile engineers and a roadmap built on platform features. The call: stay native on both platforms. Share business logic in one bounded domain with Kotlin Multiplatform, but only if a measured duplication cost justifies it. Rejected for now: a full React Native or Flutter rewrite, and Compose Multiplatform for all UI.

Revisit trigger. The cost of keeping two implementations in step, measured as parity bugs and feature lead time, rises above what we write down in the ADR. Or a platform dependency (hiring, a vendor SDK, a platform release) changes the assumption in section 4.

How I mark sources. Facts from official pages have a source and the date I read it (13 Oct 2026). Company or secondhand statements say who reported them, and I did not confirm them. Where something is my own judgement, I say so in the sentence. The sources are linked in the Sources section.

1. Why this is a decision record, not a debate

Most "native vs cross-platform" pages compare frameworks. At staff level the questions are different. Who decides? On what evidence? What would make us reverse the choice? What happens to the people and libraries left behind? An architecture decision record (ADR) writes down exactly these points. A framework comparison never gets there.

On 10 Sep 2026 Shopify published a post saying it is moving its mobile apps from React Native back to Swift and Kotlin. The company chose React Native in 2020. Its post says the original goals were met. What changed is the cost of building twice. AI coding agents now do much of the implementation, cross-platform translation, testing and review. (Reported by Shopify Engineering, 10 Sep 2026)

Gergely Orosz's coverage (The Pragmatic Engineer, 29 Sep 2026) reports that Shopify had moved all six of its mobile apps to React Native by 2025. He adds caveats. Shopify's claims are mostly self-reported. The migration is not finished. Native still means maintaining two platforms. (Reported by The Pragmatic Engineer, 29 Sep 2026) The article is paywalled. On 13 Oct 2026, only the part before its fourth section heading was readable.

I read that as one data point about one assumption, the cost of a second codebase. It is not a verdict. A decision record keeps the assumption visible, so the next reversal, in either direction, is cheaper to argue.

2. What the call looks like

The ADR template in section 10 has a slot for each part:

  • the call
  • at least three alternatives (including "do nothing" and "hybrid")
  • weighted criteria
  • the assumption that would change the decision
  • a revisit trigger with a date
  • the reversal cost

The failure I would expect most often is a record with a call and no revisit trigger. That makes a reversible choice permanent by default.

3. Criteria, weighted by company situation

Pick weights from your situation and write them down before you score the options.

CriterionWeight up whenWeight down when
Team shape and hiringYou have strong native teams, or hiring native engineers is easy in your marketYour engineers are mostly web, or you can staff only one platform
Platform-feature lag (delay before the framework supports new OS features)Your roadmap depends on new OS features, widgets or system integrationsThe app is mostly forms, lists and content
Performance ceilingHeavy animation, camera, media, large lists, tight startup targetsScreens that mainly read and write data, where network delay is the main cost
SDK and native-module needsYou depend on vendor SDKs (payments, maps, ads, identity) that ship native firstThe needed SDKs already have maintained wrappers
Code and test sharingBusiness rules are complex and must behave identically on both platformsEach platform's behavior is intentionally different
Time to first releaseA deadline matters more than the year-three costYou plan for five years
Dependency surface (how many outside libraries you rely on)You cannot staff library maintenanceYou can pay for and own your own forks (copies of a library that you maintain)
Exit costThe product may change shape within two yearsThe product is stable

The table is my synthesis. No source I read publishes weights. One reported detail fits the dependency row. Shopify's own 2025 retrospective, as quoted by Orosz, notes that the React Native framework is less comprehensive than native. So teams used more third-party libraries. (Reported by The Pragmatic Engineer, quoting Shopify's 2025 retrospective)

What the options are today (official status, 13 Oct 2026)

The landscape changes quickly, so each line carries a date and a source.

  • Kotlin Multiplatform (KMP). Google's Android documentation says it officially supports KMP for sharing business logic between Android and iOS. It calls KMP stable and production-ready. You choose how much to share, from core logic to the whole app. You can start without rebuilding the app. It lists many Jetpack libraries with KMP support: Room, Paging, DataStore, Lifecycle, Navigation and more. Platform coverage varies by library. (Source: developer.android.com/kotlin/multiplatform, 13 Oct 2026)
  • Compose Multiplatform. The Kotlin documentation's supported-platforms page lists the UI framework as stable on Android, iOS and desktop (JVM), and beta on web (Kotlin/Wasm). The same page lists core KMP as stable for Android and iOS. (Source: kotlinlang.org supported-platforms page, 13 Oct 2026)
  • React Native. Its architecture documentation says the New Architecture (JSI instead of the old bridge, concurrent rendering) is on by default in new projects since version 0.76. It also says that turning it on does not make an app faster by itself. (Source: reactnative.dev architecture page, 13 Oct 2026)

- Orosz's article reports modest gains for Shopify from the move: about 10% faster Android startup and about 3% on iOS. Some complex screens were slower until tuned. (Reported by The Pragmatic Engineer)

  • Flutter. The docs front page announced Flutter 3.47 when I read it, and the page was last updated 28 Aug 2026. Its platform-channels page says Dart talks to native Kotlin/Java and Swift/Objective-C through asynchronous message channels. These channels are not type-safe (the compiler cannot check them). The Pigeon package generates type-safe code. (Source: docs.flutter.dev, 13 Oct 2026)

Not verified here: any cross-framework performance comparison. We did not read a benchmark we trust, so this guide makes no claim about which framework is faster. Measure your own screens (section 9).

4. The assumption that would change the decision

Every decision rests on one or two assumptions. They are cheaper to monitor than to argue again. Write them down.

As I read the Shopify post, it does not claim React Native failed. It says React Native delivered what it was adopted for: building features once, letting developers without mobile backgrounds contribute, and less time spent on parity. Its costs also grew: performance tuning, framework upgrades and dependency management. What changed was the price of the second codebase. (Reported by Shopify Engineering, 10 Sep 2026)

In record form, the 2020 decision had an unwritten assumption: "writing both platforms costs more than sharing". The 2026 reversal rests on that assumption failing.

For your record, write the assumption in this shape:

  • Assumption: "Building and keeping two native implementations costs more than owning one cross-platform stack, measured as engineer-weeks per feature."
  • Evidence today: the number, with its date and source.
  • Trigger: "If the measured ratio crosses X, or a named capability ships, reopen this record."
  • Owner and date: a person and a review date, not a team.

The easy mistake in late 2026 is to swap one untested assumption for the opposite one. "Agents make two codebases cheap" is, today, a claim reported by the company making the move, and its migration is unfinished. Treat it as a hypothesis (an idea to test). Measure it on one app first. That is the purpose of a pilot (section 6).

5. Greenfield or brownfield

There are two migration shapes, and the choice belongs in the decision.

Brownfield: convert the existing app in place, screen by screen or module by module. The app keeps shipping. But for months you live with two worlds in one binary (the built app file), interop code (code that connects the two worlds) and two navigation models.

KMP is designed for this shape. Google's page says you can share logic without a complete rewrite. (Source: developer.android.com/kotlin/multiplatform, 13 Oct 2026) Most surprises happen in the interop between the two worlds.

Greenfield: build the new app beside the old one, then cut over. Shopify chose this, per its post. Its reasons: agents can port features using the existing React Native app as a reference, and a clean start avoids old constraints.

It did the Shop app first (a proof of concept to a published native app in 12 weeks, per the post). It is working on the Shopify app (over 300 screens, expected later in 2026). It plans the other apps after that. These are the company's own statements, and the work was in progress when I read them. (Reported by Shopify Engineering, 10 Sep 2026)

A simple guide: choose brownfield when the existing app is healthy and the pain is local (one domain duplicated, one team blocked). Choose greenfield only when the old stack itself is the problem, and you can afford to run both for a quarter or more.

In both cases, start with the smallest app that has real users, as Shopify did with the Shop app. Write the expected duration before you start, so you can see when it slips.

6. Parity testing

When two implementations must behave the same, the tests are the contract.

The Shopify post gives the pattern at the high end. Each rebuilt screen must pass tests, match the running app visually, pass review steps and get human approval before the next one starts. Business logic was redesigned to be separate from the UI. This lets it run headlessly (without a screen). (Reported by Shopify Engineering, 10 Sep 2026)

Orosz reports that the shared business-logic test suite is central, because it gives agents fast feedback. (Reported by The Pragmatic Engineer, 29 Sep 2026)

You do not need agents to use the idea. Here is a parity setup I would put in a staff-level plan. Parity means that both platforms behave the same way.

  • Shared behavior spec. Write business rules as data-driven test cases (inputs and expected outputs) in a neutral format. Both platforms' test suites run them. A bug found on one platform becomes a case for both.
  • Contract tests at the boundary. If you share logic through KMP, test the exported API from Swift as well as from Kotlin. This is how iOS developers will call it.
  • Screenshot comparison per screen. Allow the platform differences you accept, and be strict about the rest. Write the accepted differences down.
  • Instrumentation parity. Use the same events and the same names on both sides. A funnel with a different definition on each platform is not a measurement.
  • A pilot with a rollback. A pilot is a small first trial. Ship the first migrated screen to a small group of users. Compare metrics against the old screen (section 9).

Parity tests are cheap to start and expensive to add later. Write the first ten cases before you write the first migrated screen.

7. Who owns the orphaned libraries

Any framework choice leaves libraries behind: community forks, internal modules, open-source projects you started. If you reverse the choice, these libraries become orphans (libraries with no owner) unless someone decides otherwise.

Shopify's post shows three different outcomes, which gives a useful checklist. (Reported by Shopify Engineering, 10 Sep 2026)

  • Skia for React Native is sponsored through the end of 2026. Its maintainer plans a fork under a new name. The original repository will be archived after the transition.
  • FlashList, described as downloaded about two million times a week, gets critical compatibility fixes from Shopify. Shopify talks to companies about long-term stewardship (who will look after it).
  • Restyle is archived and kept working through the end of 2026. Forks are welcome.

So the options are: sponsor a handover, look for stewards, or archive with notice. For each internal or open-source library of your own, decide in the record:

  1. Who are the users, inside and outside the company? Count them if you can.
  2. Who owns it after the migration, by name? "The platform team" is not a name. It is a risk.
  3. What is the sunset date (the date support ends), and what do consumers do on that date?
  4. What is the exit path: fork, transfer, archive, or keep funding.

Put the library question into the decision, not after it. A rewrite plan that does not say what happens to the shared libraries hides the cost that is easiest to postpone.

See our pagination library case study. It treats deprecation windows, binary compatibility and version-adoption telemetry (data on which app versions people use) as part of a library's design. See also the question of build or adopt in the image loading case study.

8. Rollback and reversibility on mobile

A web deploy can be reversed in minutes. A mobile release cannot. That changes how you plan a framework change.

What you can undo

  • Stop a rollout. A staged rollout is a release to a growing share of users. On Google Play, halting a staged rollout stops the update from reaching more users. You can raise the percentage by hand. (Source: Google Play Console help, 13 Oct 2026)
  • Pause a phased release. On the App Store, a phased release for automatic updates runs over seven days at 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 percent. It can be paused for up to 30 days in total. (Source: App Store Connect help, 13 Oct 2026)
  • Turn a feature off. Server-controlled flags let you disable a code path without a store release. This works only if the code path shipped with a flag. A kill switch is a remote setting that turns a feature off. See the kill-switch discussion in our analytics SDK and push SDK case studies.

What you cannot undo

  • Users who already have the build. Google's help page says that anyone who already received the update keeps it when you halt. It does not describe a rollback option. Its suggested fix is a new release with a corrected bundle. (Source: Google Play Console help, 13 Oct 2026)
  • Manual updaters. Apple's page says people who update manually are not held back by the phased schedule. (Source: App Store Connect help, 13 Oct 2026)
  • Old clients. Users who never update keep running old versions. So any server contract, data format or deep link that you change must keep working with them. The push SDK case study designs for this.
  • On-device data. A migrated database or a changed cache format cannot be reverted by turning off a flag. The new build must be able to read what the old build wrote. If you plan a rollback by shipping the old code again, the old build must also read what the new build wrote.

The rollback ladder for a framework migration (reversal steps, from fastest to slowest):

StepReversalCost
Per-screen flag, both implementations in the binaryA server flag switches back within minutes for users on the buildLarger app size and double maintenance while the flag exists
Staged rollout of the new app versionPause the rollout; users who already have it keep itFast to stop, slow to fully recall
Ship the old code as a new releaseA new store release; the version number moves forward, not backDays, plus review and rollout time
Cutover of a greenfield appKeep the old app in the stores for a stated periodTwo apps to maintain

Before you start, decide how long each step stays available, and write it in the record. Decide "we are not going back after date D" before D, when it costs least.

9. What to measure and the guardrails

Before the first group of users sees the change, pick one success metric, a few guardrails (metrics that must not get worse) and a stopping rule.

  • Success metric. The one that justified the decision, for example feature lead time (merge to production), parity bugs per release, or engineer-weeks per feature across both platforms. Define it so you can read it before and after.
  • Guardrails. Watch crash-free sessions, and ANR (app not responding) and hang rates. Watch cold start (time to open the app from nothing) and jank (slow or uneven drawing of frames). Also watch app size and the conversion step. For definitions and field data, use the Android vitals and Apple MetricKit documentation, not a blog's thresholds. I did not verify specific vitals thresholds, so none are quoted.
  • Comparison method. Compare the migrated screen against the old screen in the same time window and the same group of users. Watch for the effect that Meta's 2013 Messenger post warned about. It is an unexplained improvement that is really a logging change. (Reported by Facebook Engineering, 13 Nov 2013) I did not fetch this post again for this guide. It is old, so use it as a pattern.
  • Stopping rule. "If crash-free sessions on the migrated group fall by more than the tolerance we wrote down, we pause the rollout." Write the tolerance. Do not leave it as "meaningfully".
  • Cost side. Track engineer-weeks and compare with your estimate at checkpoints such as 25%, 50% and 75% (example marks). Without checkpoints on the estimate, you learn how big the choice was only at the end.

For a model of a measured adoption decision, use the Uber Android team's Kotlin build study as a template. It ran many experiments across configurations. It surveyed engineers. It gave guidance with caveats, not a flat yes or no. (Reported by Uber Engineering, 30 Apr 2019) I did not fetch this study again for this guide. For the same measurement shape, see our staff mobile loop guide, section 4.

11. Android and iOS specifics

  • Android. Google documents Android Studio support and a migration route for adding KMP to an existing app. It also lists the Jetpack libraries with KMP support. Check which platforms your library version covers. (Source: developer.android.com/kotlin/multiplatform, 13 Oct 2026)
  • iOS. Shared Kotlin reaches Swift through a generated framework. So the API that Swift developers see is part of your design. I did not read the Apple and JetBrains interop docs for this guide. The Kotlin compatibility page lists Xcode 26.4 in its newest row. This is a reminder that toolchain versions are a cost that comes back again and again. (Source: kotlinlang.org compatibility guide, 13 Oct 2026)
  • Stores. Apple pauses a phased release for up to 30 days in total. Google lets you pause and set the percentage by hand (section 8).

12. A spoken outline of a staff answer

Use this as a shape for a conversation about the decision, in a design discussion or any other place where you must defend a framework choice. It is my synthesis, not a script for any specific process. I do not know what any company asks.

  1. Frame (30 seconds). "I want to treat this as a decision we may have to defend and possibly reverse. So I will state the call, the alternatives, and what would change my mind."
  2. Ask. "How many apps and engineers? Which platforms? How heavy is the roadmap on platform features? How is hiring going? What started the question?"
  3. Weights. "For a team like this, I would give high weight to platform-feature lag and SDK needs, and low weight to time to first release. Tell me if the weights are wrong, because the answer changes with them."
  4. The call and the alternatives. "My call is [X]. I considered [Y] and [Z], and did not choose them because [reason tied to the weights]."
  5. The assumption. "The assumption I am least sure about is [cost of a second codebase, or hiring, or a vendor SDK]. I would put a number on it and a date to check it."
  6. Migration. "Brownfield or greenfield, with the smallest real scope first, an estimate and checkpoints."
  7. Parity and ownership. "A shared behavior spec runs on both platforms. Each library gets a named owner and a sunset date."
  8. Rollback. "Flags per screen and a staged store rollout. Also an honest list of what I cannot undo: users who already have the build, manual updaters, old clients and migrated local data."
  9. Measure. "One success metric, guardrails with tolerances, a stop rule, and a cost checkpoint at a quarter, half and three quarters."
  10. Close. "I would write this as an ADR with a review date, so the next person can see why we chose it and when to reopen it."

The close matters most. Senior answers tend to stop at the call. The written, dated, reversible record is what makes an answer staff-level.
