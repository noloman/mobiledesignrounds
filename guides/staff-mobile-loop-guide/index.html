Staff mobile engineer interview: what the system design round tests

Last reviewed 14 Oct 2026. Drafted with AI assistance. Checked against official documentation where noted (how this is checked). Part of: The five-step method.

TL;DR. Public sources say the round moves from "can you build it" to "can you decide what to build and ship it safely". At staff level, you lead and cover rollout, risk, measurement, privacy and ownership. We cannot tell you what any company asks. This guide keeps public sources apart from our own view. It has a worked example and a four-week plan built on our ten case studies.

1. What changes from senior to staff

Two public sources describe level expectations for this round, and they point the same way. The ByteByteGo course introduction says seniors should lead the talk and explain their trade-offs. It says Staff+ candidates should also weigh business impact and risk. It is a commercial course, so it is not an official source.

The weeeBox guide says the staff-level talk turns strategic. It adds that how long you have worked is only a loose hint about level, not a rule.

Outside mobile, one published engineering ladder (Foursquare's) makes a related point in general terms. It describes its staff level as a multi-team or product leader. It says that level sets technical direction and helps define requirements. It calls this a different job from the level below, not just more of the same. That is a company document about one ladder, not a rule for interviews.

Our synthesis, built on those sources and on the "What changes by level" sections of our case studies, is six shifts:

  • Scope. A senior answer designs the feature. A staff answer also decides which parts are a shared module, an SDK (a code library other developers add to their apps), or another team's service.
  • Unclear requirements. The staff candidate narrows the problem on purpose. They say who the users are, which platforms count, what is out of scope, and why.
  • Trade-offs across teams. "Build or adopt" and "who owns this" are part of the design, not a late addition.
  • Risk. What can fail, how you notice, and how you limit the damage.
  • Rollout. Flags, release to a growing share of users, support for old app versions, and migration.
  • Organization. Clear module boundaries, so several teams can work without blocking each other.

2. What interviewers can observe

We have no insider view of how any company scores this round. What follows is a list of behaviors you can see, and it is our synthesis. An interviewer can only grade what happens in the room. So ask which behavior, at which moment, would show the shift.

First 10 minutes (framing)

  • You ask about users, platforms and scale. Then you say your assumptions out loud and ask if they hold.
  • You cut scope on purpose ("I will cover send and sync, and leave search out") and name the risk of what you cut.
  • You ask what success looks like and how to measure it before you draw boxes.

The hard part (the deep dive)

  • You raise failure modes before anyone asks: the app is killed, a response is lost, a message arrives twice, old app versions still run.
  • You give two options for a decision and say what each costs. You pick one, with a reason tied to the stated constraints.
  • The interviewer adds a constraint. You change the design and say what moved, instead of defending the first draft.
  • You name where the design touches another team or service, and what contract you want from it.

Wrap-up (last 5 to 10 minutes)

  • You describe how you would ship it: the flag (a remote switch), the group of users, the metric to watch, and the condition that triggers a rollback.
  • You say what you would measure and which gaps you cannot observe.
  • You list what you would do in the second quarter, not only the first release.

Suppose you show the first-10-minutes behaviors but never reach the wrap-up. The round can still read as senior. That is a hypothesis from our reading, not a finding.

3. Worked example: the lost acknowledgement in a chat app

The sub-problem: the user taps send, the request reaches the server, and the response is lost. The app does not know if the message arrived.

Our chat app case study designs it this way:

  • The app inserts the message into Room (Android's on-device database) with a pending status and a clientId that the client creates.
  • A unique WorkManager job (Android's scheduler for work that survives restarts) sends it.
  • The server is idempotent on clientId. This means doing the same request twice has the same result as doing it once.
  • The retry returns the original serverId and seq. It does not create a second message.

The case study's level section lists what it expects at each level. The three answers below are our illustration of how that could sound.

Mid-level answer. "I save the message locally, show it, and send it in the background. If it fails, I retry." This is a working send flow with a retry. The gap: it does not say what happens when the failure is a lost response. The retry may then create a duplicate.

Senior answer. "The client creates a clientId, and the server treats it as an idempotency key. So a retry after a lost acknowledgement returns the original message."

"Pending messages have no seq yet. I show confirmed messages by seq and pending ones by creation time. Retries use exponential backoff (wait longer after each failure), with jitter (random variation in each delay) on socket reconnects. A permanent 4xx error marks the message failed, with a manual retry."

Note the specific points: the key, the order, the backoff, and which errors stop retries. The case study also expects a senior to mention Doze (the deep-sleep mode of an idle phone) and battery use without being asked.

Staff answer. Everything above, plus the layers around it:

  • Measurement. "I would track time to acknowledgement, failed-send rate, retry rate and duplicate rate. A rising duplicate rate means the idempotency contract is broken somewhere."
  • Rollout. "A change to the send path goes behind a flag and reaches a small group of users first. A bug here corrupts conversations."
  • Contract. "The idempotency window and how long the server keeps clientId are a contract with the backend team. Old app versions must keep working."
  • Multi-device and encryption. "With several devices, the server stays the authority on order. End-to-end encryption changes what the server can check for duplicates or search."
  • Ownership. "Send, sync and display sit in separate modules, so more than one team can own them."

The staff answer is not longer for its own sake. It moves the talk from "does it work" to "how do we know it works in production, and who maintains it".

4. Staff-level topics most mobile answers skip

Each topic below has a case study that works through it. The pointers come from the "What changes by level" sections of those case studies.

  • Rollout and kill switches. A kill switch (a remote setting that turns a feature off) and release to a growing share of users: see the analytics SDK and push SDK cases. Flags around changes to the payment path: booking app. Player changes compared between groups of users: video app.
  • Observability and metrics. Delivery time and retry rates are in the chat app. Time to first content and slow frames are in the news feed. Gap and resync rate are in the live ticker. Drop counts by reason are in the analytics SDK.
  • Ownership by several teams, and modules. The news feed has a shared feed module. The booking app has search, checkout and trips as separate modules. The video app has a shared playback module.
  • Support for old versions, and SDK versions. The pagination library covers binary compatibility, deprecation windows and data on which versions people use. The push SDK covers a stable API across years of app versions. Image loading covers a stable API and a chain of interceptors.
  • Cost of changes. The live ticker has the cost model of a feed (fan-out, subscription limits). The video app compares CDN bytes with engagement. Photo and file sync covers backend cost and the rollout of a new chunk size. Image loading and the pagination library cover the question of build or adopt.
  • Testing strategy. Fake APIs can drop the response after a success, force error states and send events out of order. See the follow-up questions in the booking app and live ticker. The pagination library covers how to test a library itself.
  • Privacy and consent. The analytics SDK covers consent checks, deletion on opt-out and store disclosures. The push SDK covers consent and remote kill switches. Photo and file sync covers duplicate detection, privacy and end-to-end encryption.

5. A four-week practice plan

Follow this rule in every session. Speak aloud, to a recording or a peer. Stop at 45 minutes. State your assumptions at the start and again when you change them.

The 45 minutes is our choice for practice. The weeeBox guide describes a 45 to 60 minute round, where the deep discussion takes 20 to 30 minutes. Formats vary.

  1. Week 1: framing and the core loop. Do the chat app and the news feed. Focus on the first 10 minutes: scope, assumptions and success metrics. Then answer the case study's follow-up questions without reading them first.
  2. Week 2: systems that fail. Do photo and file sync and the live ticker. Focus on failure modes and on what the operating system will and will not run in the background. End each session with a two-minute summary of rollout and metrics.
  3. Week 3: libraries and SDKs. Do the pagination library, image loading, analytics SDK and push SDK, one or two sessions each. Focus on stable APIs, versions, build or adopt, and consent.
  4. Week 4: integrated products and mixed review. Do the booking app and the video app, then repeat one earlier case with a changed constraint. For example, add end-to-end encryption, or add a second app that must reuse the module. Listen to your recordings. Count how many of the staff topics in section 4 you raised without being asked.

6. Common mistakes

These are our observations from the public guidance and from how the case studies are built. They are not data about candidates.

  • Spending the whole round on architecture layers and never reaching rollout or metrics.
  • Treating "staff" as "more components" instead of "more decisions with stated reasons".
  • Skipping scope and assumptions, then designing a product nobody described.
  • Presenting one option as the only option.
  • Saying "feature flag" without saying what you would watch and what starts a rollback.
  • Claiming metrics you cannot measure, such as push delivery, without naming the gaps you cannot see.
  • Recommending a custom library without asking whether to build or adopt.
  • Ignoring old app versions when you propose a change to a protocol or schema.
  • Freezing when the interviewer adds a constraint, instead of saying what changes.

7. What we do not know

  • Company loops vary. The weeeBox guide itself says the interview structure depends on the interviewer's style. We have not verified what any specific company asks at staff level. We do not claim to.
  • We have no insider data. The author of this site has not run these interviews. The author does not claim to know how loops are scored.
  • Stories are only stories. Forum posts about specific loops are one person's claim. They are often years old. They do not tell you how any loop works today. This guide cites none.
  • Level names differ between companies. The ladder we cite is from one company. "Staff" can mean different things elsewhere.
  • From public sources: the level expectations in section 1. These come from the ByteByteGo introduction (a commercial course, not an official source), the weeeBox guide and one company ladder. Our synthesis: the six shifts, the behavior signals in section 2, the three illustrative answers in section 3, the practice plan and the mistakes list.
  • No promises. Preparing this way does not guarantee any result in any process.
